# Interview — Marcus Webb, QA Reviewer (SYNTHETIC)

*Synthetic transcript. Persona P2, tenure 6 years. Generated per data/interviews/SYNTHETIC.md.*

**Interviewer:** Walk me through a standard onboarding — from where you sit.

**Marcus:** I see it after the fact. I pull a sample of closed cases and re-walk every step against the written procedure: identifiers collected, identity verified, screening run and dispositioned, risk rating supported, beneficial ownership certified where applicable, decision documented. My job is boring on a good day. It has not been boring lately.

**Interviewer:** What does the manual not tell you?

**Marcus:** The manual is actually fine. The gap is between the manual and the floor. Take proof of address. The written standard says a utility bill within 90 days is acceptable. The senior analysts run their own 60 days rule — anything older gets bounced back to the customer. I've stopped writing it up as a finding because technically stricter isn't non-compliant, but now the customer experience depends on which analyst you draw. That's a consistency problem, and consistency is the whole point of a written procedure.

**Interviewer:** Which written steps get skipped in practice?

**Marcus:** The phone callback verification. It's in the procedure for a reason — non-documentary verification when we can't fully rely on the documents. In my last two quarterly samples, for accounts under $10k expected activity, the callback gets skipped in most files. Under ten thousand, no callback, straight to decision. Nobody wrote that rule down; it just grew. When I raise it, I hear "risk-based approach." A risk-based approach you invented on the floor and never documented is not a risk-based approach, it's a habit with good PR.

**Interviewer:** Describe a case that went wrong.

**Marcus:** A PO box case, naturally. Applicant gives a PO box as their address. One of our frontline reps accepted it with a supplemental document. My read is the opposite: a PO box is not a residential address, full stop — I reject those files at review and send them back for a physical address. The rep's read is that policy doesn't literally forbid it, and he's technically right, because the policy is silent. So the same fact pattern is a rejection in my queue and an approval in his. We've argued about it at two team meetings. It's still unresolved, which drives me up a wall — I don't even care which rule we pick, I care that there's a rule.

**Interviewer:** When do you escalate?

**Marcus:** When a finding repeats. One skipped step is coaching; the same skipped step across nine files is a process, and processes that contradict the manual go up the chain in writing.

**Interviewer:** Which rules do you apply more strictly than written?

**Marcus:** None. That's almost the job description. The standard is the standard — my opinions about it go into the annual procedure review like everyone else's should.

**Interviewer:** Anything else?

**Marcus:** The sampling tool caps me at fixed sample sizes and I'd kill for risk-weighted sampling. And the checklist template still says "photocopy" in 2026. Neither of those is a control failure; they're just things that make the job slower than it needs to be.
